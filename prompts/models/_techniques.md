## Prompting rules

1. **State the goal, output shape, and constraints explicitly — never assume the model infers them.** Underspecified prompts get a plausible guess ~41% of the time, but those guesses are inconsistent across models/versions: underspecified prompts are 2x more likely to regress on a prompt or model change, sometimes losing 20+ accuracy points. [paper]
2. **Give the reason behind a constraint, not just the constraint.** Vendor guidance converges here (OpenAI's "Decision Drivers" — 3-5 bullets explaining *why* — replacing scripted CoT; Anthropic's "tell Claude why, not just what"), but no controlled ablation isolating this exact effect was found — treat as strong practitioner consensus, not a measured result. [vendor]
3. **Phrase constraints positively ("keep it under 200 words") instead of negatively ("don't be verbose").** LLMs process a negated concept before suppressing it and frequently mishandle the suppression: one audit found a model endorsing "they should rob the store" 80% of the time despite an explicit "should NOT" in the prompt. Anthropic's docs independently recommend positive framing. [paper][vendor]
4. **Use 1-3 few-shot examples when output format/style must be exact — more is often wasted tokens.** Current vendor guidance for reasoning models is "zero-shot by default, add ≤2 examples to show format" (OpenAI); Anthropic recommends wrapping examples in tags and, for extended-thinking models, showing the reasoning style inside the example itself. This is a scaled-down descendant of the original few-shot result (8 examples lifted GSM8K substantially on PaLM 540B) — the lift is real but concentrated on format-matching now, not raw accuracy, on frontier reasoning models. [paper][vendor]
5. **Decompose multi-step tasks into ordered sub-steps instead of one monolithic ask.** Decomposed Prompting outperformed few-shot and chain-of-thought baselines on compositional tasks by giving each sub-step its own focused prompt; the advantage grows as task complexity grows. [paper]
6. **Specify the output schema (JSON/XML tags) when a downstream step will parse the result.** Structured tags measurably reduce ambiguity and raise schema-compliance, but schema compliance is a different metric from field-level correctness — structured-output benchmarks show schema validity can rise while per-field accuracy falls. Verify field values separately; don't treat "valid JSON" as "correct JSON." [paper]
7. **Role/persona framing helps for style and domain framing, not as a factual-accuracy lever.** Across 27 tasks and 9 models, expert personas improved extraction (+0.65), STEM (+0.60), and reasoning (+0.40) categories but degraded three categories needing precise fact retrieval; a separate controlled study found domain-tailored personas did not generally improve factual accuracy, and irrelevant persona details can cost up to 30 accuracy points. Use personas to set tone/audience, not to boost correctness. [paper]
8. **For high-stakes single-answer reasoning, sample N times and vote/judge rather than trusting one greedy generation.** Self-consistency (multiple reasoning paths, majority vote) reliably beats single-shot decoding, and the improvement scales with sample count — this is the mechanism behind self-consistency and best-of-N methods generally. [paper][practitioner]
9. **On tasks with a cheap, automatable eval metric, run an automatic prompt optimizer instead of hand-tuning.** DSPy-style optimizers explore thousands of variants a human can't in a focused session; case studies report 10-40% quality gains over manual prompts (one: 46.2%→64.0% accuracy), and PE2 (LLM-driven meta-prompting) beat "let's think step by step" by 3.1-6.3 points on math benchmarks. The catch: this needs a metric to optimize against, so it fits classification/extraction/routing far better than open-ended writing. [paper]
10. **Don't stack more than ~5-6 hard constraints in a single prompt.** Constraint-satisfaction research on long-form generation found compliance drops once a prompt passes roughly 6 constraints; split into decomposed steps or a multi-turn refinement instead of one all-in-one instruction. [paper]
11. **For agentic/tool-use prompts, tell the model when *not* to use a tool, not just what the tool does.** Anthropic's applied guidance: unambiguous, single-purpose tool descriptions with explicit non-use conditions produce large reliability gains; a survey of agentic tool use independently documents that tool selection in LLM agents is measurably unreliable without this. [vendor][paper]
12. **Treat prompt-template formatting (Markdown vs. YAML vs. JSON, delimiter choice) as a real variable on small/weak models, a minor one on frontier models.** GPT-3.5-class models swing up to 40% in accuracy purely from template choice; GPT-4-class models are markedly more robust. Pick one template and hold it constant for reproducibility, but don't assume the exact template matters much once you're on a frontier model. [paper]
13. **Tone and politeness are not a reliable lever — don't spend effort tuning them.** Effects are small, model-dependent, and domain-dependent: one 2024 study found rudeness slightly *raised* accuracy (80.8%→84.8%); a 2025 follow-up found rude tone hurt GPT/Llama specifically on humanities tasks while Gemini stayed tone-insensitive. Net effect across mixed-domain use is noise-level. [paper]
14. **Match reasoning-effort/thinking-budget controls to task difficulty as a separate knob from prompt structure — fix structure first, then tune effort.** This is now explicit vendor guidance (OpenAI: treat reasoning effort as "a last-mile knob, not the primary way to improve quality"), reflecting that native reasoning models manage their own reasoning depth via a dedicated parameter rather than through prompt text. [vendor]

## Controls

| Technique | Use when | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Few-shot examples (1-3, format-matching) | Output format/style must be exact; classification, extraction, style transfer | Vendor guidance (OpenAI: ≤2 examples; Anthropic: tag-wrapped examples, reasoning-style shown inline for thinking models) [vendor] |
| Explicit decomposition into ordered sub-steps | Multi-hop reasoning, agent workflows, tasks with dependent stages, long-form writing | DecomP outperforms few-shot/CoT baselines on compositional tasks; advantage grows with task complexity [paper] |
| Self-consistency / N-sample + vote | High-stakes single-answer reasoning or math where extra calls are affordable | Self-consistency majority-vote beats greedy decoding; gains scale with N [paper][practitioner] |
| Automatic prompt optimization (DSPy / APE / PE2-style meta-prompting) | Task has a cheap automatic metric and will run repeatedly/at scale | 10-40% gains reported in DSPy case studies; original APE matched/beat human prompts on 24/24 instruction-induction tasks, 17/21 curated BIG-Bench tasks; PE2 beat "think step by step" by 3.1-6.3 pts on math [paper] |
| Output schema specification (JSON/XML tags) | A downstream step parses the result programmatically | Raises schema-compliance reliably; field-level accuracy is a separate, unresolved axis — verify it separately [paper] |
| Positive-only phrasing of constraints | Any hard constraint you actually need enforced | Negation is unreliably processed by LLMs; documented cases of models acting against explicit "NOT" instructions [paper] |
| Persona/role assignment | Tone, audience framing, extraction/STEM/reasoning-category tasks — not raw factual accuracy | +0.4-0.65 on extraction/STEM/reasoning categories; degrades 3 fact-retrieval categories; up to -30 pts with irrelevant persona detail; Dec-2025 study found no general factual-accuracy gain [paper] |
| Native reasoning-effort/thinking-budget parameter | Task genuinely needs deep multi-step reasoning, on a model that exposes this control | Vendor guidance: tune as a "last-mile" knob after prompt structure is fixed, not the primary quality lever [vendor] |
| Tool descriptions stating when *not* to use a tool | Any agentic/tool-use prompt | Small wording refinements to tool descriptions produce large agent reliability gains; tool selection is otherwise measurably unreliable [vendor][paper] |

## Anti-patterns

- **Explicit "think step by step" / hand-written CoT scaffolding on native reasoning models** — died because o-series, Claude extended thinking, and Gemini thinking models already generate a hidden internal reasoning trace before answering. A controlled 4,950-run-per-model study found reasoning models gained only 2.9-3.1% from explicit CoT (one model, Gemini Flash 2.5, *declined* 3.3%) for 20-80% extra latency — versus 4.4-13.5% gains (with one outright regression) on non-reasoning models. Replaced by native reasoning-effort/thinking-budget parameters and compact "why" bullets instead of scripted reasoning steps.
- **Threats ("you will be punished/fired if you fail")** — died on a controlled study (4,950 runs/model, GPQA + MMLU-Pro) that found no net benchmark effect despite Sergey Brin's widely circulated claim that models "do better if you threaten them." Per-question swings were as likely to hurt (-35 pts) as help (+36 pts), canceling out. Replaced by plain direct instructions.
- **Tipping ("I'll give you $200 for a great answer")** — same study, same null result. An independent practitioner analysis (Max Woolf, 2024) reached the same conclusion earlier and in more detail. Replaced by nothing — just state the request.
- **Elaborate expert personas as an accuracy lever** — died specifically for factual/reasoning tasks: a December 2025 controlled study ("Playing Pretend") found domain-tailored personas did not generally improve accuracy, and low-knowledge personas ("you are a toddler") could actively hurt it. Replaced by direct instructions for correctness, with persona kept only for tone/style framing (where it still helps).
- **Negative-instruction-first constraint writing ("don't do X", "never Y", "avoid Z" stacked)** — replaced by positive phrasing. Multiple papers document that negation triggers a represent-then-suppress process LLMs handle unreliably, up to and including a documented case of an open model endorsing "rob the store" 80% of the time despite an explicit "should NOT."
- **Giant single mega-prompt stacking many hard constraints** — replaced by decomposition or multi-turn refinement once a prompt passes roughly 6 simultaneous hard constraints, per constrained long-form generation research; compliance falls off past that point regardless of model size.
- **ALL-CAPS / emphasis-stacking ("CRITICAL: YOU MUST ALWAYS...")** — no controlled study isolating caps-stacking specifically was found in this research pass; the closest evidence is prompt-formatting-sensitivity work showing frontier models are comparatively robust to surface formatting while weaker models swing up to 40% on template choice. Anthropic's own model-migration guidance for its latest models explicitly says to *dial back* this kind of booster language because current models over-trigger on it. Treat the specific "caps helps" folklore claim as unproven either way, but the trend among vendors is unambiguously away from it.
- **Manual-only prompt engineering for tasks that run repeatedly at scale** — being displaced by automatic optimizers (DSPy, APE, PE2-style meta-prompting) wherever a cheap eval metric exists, because they search far more variants than a human can (a human explores roughly 20-50 variations in a session; an optimizer evaluates thousands). Still needs a human-written seed prompt and a metric to optimize against — it does not remove the human from tasks like open-ended creative writing that have no such metric.

## Task-shape playbook

| Task shape | What to do | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Classification | 2-3 few-shot examples spanning real edge cases (not just clean cases); if run at scale, optimize the prompt automatically against a held-out labeled set | Long persona setup; explicit CoT preambles — they add latency without moving labels much on reasoning models |
| Extraction | Explicit output schema (JSON/XML) with a field-by-field description, including one example that shows a null/missing-field case | Trusting "valid JSON" as "correct JSON" — schema compliance and field accuracy are separate axes; verify values independently |
| Long-form writing | Plan/outline first (decomposition), then generate section by section against the outline; state audience, length, and constraints once, up front | Stacking more than ~6 hard constraints in one shot; expecting single-shot generation to hold structure over very long output |
| Code generation | Spec → plan → implement, in that order; state target language, interfaces, and acceptance criteria explicitly | Vague "make it better/cleaner" asks with no criteria; bolting an explicit "think step by step" preamble onto a reasoning-tuned code model — let its native reasoning run instead |
| Agentic / tool-use | Single-purpose tool descriptions that state when *not* to use each tool; explicit stop conditions and a minimal tool set | Ambiguous or overlapping tool descriptions; assuming more tools available = more capability — tool selection in agentic LLMs is documented as unreliable without careful description design |
| Creative / open-ended | Persona and constraint prompting to diversify voice; outline-first ("plan-and-write") for anything beyond short-form; accept multiple candidate generations as a feature, not noise | Trying to run automatic prompt optimization against a single "best" output — creative tasks generally lack the clean metric these optimizers need |

## Spoken-idea → prompt

This is the exact shape of Sanele's prompt mode: a rambling voice transcript comes in, a clean optimized prompt must come out — with no back-and-forth to ask the speaker for clarification. Two directly relevant HCI studies (Rambler, CHI'24; StepWrite, UIST'25) and the underspecification/negation literature above converge on a specific playbook:

1. **Decompose the rewrite into stages — don't ask one model call to clean the transcript *and* write the final prompt.** Rambler's core finding was that flat "transcribe then hand to an LLM" loses structure that speech never had in the first place; StepWrite's decomposition into planned sub-steps produced output "more closely aligned with users' intended meaning" than flat transcription, with over 77% of its intermediate scaffolding judged essential to the final result. Extract gist first, draft second.
2. **Separate the disfluency-strip pass from the intent-extraction pass.** Speech is systematically wordier, more repetitive, and less linear than typed text — that's the documented reason speech-to-writing tools need heavy post-processing, not a transcription-quality problem. Don't try to fix grammar and infer intent in the same reasoning step.
3. **Resolve self-correction by recency, not averaging.** When a speaker says "make it formal — actually no, keep it casual, formal is too stiff for this," take the *last* stated preference. Rambling speech routinely revises itself out loud; a rewrite that blends both statements produces neither.
4. **Reconstruct explicit structure the speaker never stated out loud, but don't invent values they never gave.** Underspecification is the single most-documented failure mode in prompt writing generally (see rule 1) and speech is worse than typed text here — people don't self-edit for completeness while talking. Make the goal, format, and constraints explicit in the output prompt. But if the speaker never said a length, audience, or tone, leave that dimension open rather than guessing a specific value — a guessed default is worse than an omitted one, because guesses are inconsistent across runs and silently narrow what the downstream model will do.
5. **Convert spoken negative asides into positive constraints.** A rambling "don't make it sound too corporate" should become "keep the tone casual/direct" in the rewritten prompt — not preserved as a negation — because the model that eventually executes the rewritten prompt will process that constraint more reliably in positive form (see rule 3/anti-patterns).
6. **Preserve any reason the speaker gave for a constraint, verbatim in substance.** If the transcript contains "...because the client is picky about formality," keep that justification attached to the constraint in the output prompt rather than dropping it — a stated reason is exactly what lets a downstream reasoning model generalize the rule to cases the speaker didn't enumerate (rule 2).
7. **Keep any example the speaker gave verbally, don't paraphrase it away.** "Something like the email I sent yesterday" or a phrase they spoke aloud as a model to imitate is a de facto few-shot example — flag it as an example in the rewritten prompt rather than summarizing it into a generic style description, since concrete examples are consistently the strongest lever for steering output shape (rule 4).
8. **Treat trailing hedges as low-confidence noise, not requirements.** Filler like "...I don't know, whatever works" or "...something like that, yeah" signals the speaker has no real requirement there — drop it rather than encoding it as a constraint the downstream model must satisfy.
9. **One clean pass through this pipeline, not an open-ended rewrite loop.** Both Rambler and StepWrite beat flat "speech-to-text + ask ChatGPT to fix it" baselines specifically through *structured, staged* processing (gist-extraction-then-manipulation; plan-then-generate) rather than unstructured iterative prompting — this validates Sanele's existing deconstruct-then-build framing in `sanele-prompt.sh` as the right shape, not just a stylistic choice.
10. **Where Best mode (multi-candidate + judge) is available, prefer it for the prompt-rewrite step itself when the speaker's intent is genuinely ambiguous from the transcript alone.** This mirrors the general self-consistency finding (rule 8/Controls) — sampling multiple candidate rewrites and selecting/merging is a documented way to raise reliability on exactly the kind of single-shot judgment call ("what did they actually mean") that a rambling transcript forces.

## Inject
(Compact digest — this is the ONLY section injected into a live prompt. Keep it under ~900 chars; the sections above are human reference.)
- State the goal, the output shape, and the real constraints explicitly. Underspecified prompts get a plausible guess and regress badly across model changes.
- Phrase constraints positively ("keep it under 200 words") rather than negatively ("don't be verbose") — models handle negation unreliably.
- Keep it to about six hard constraints or fewer; past that, compliance measurably falls off. Decompose instead.
- Use 1-3 short examples only when the output format must be exact.
- Use a role/persona to set tone and audience, not as a correctness lever.
- Decompose genuinely multi-step tasks into ordered sub-steps.
- Never use threats, tipping, or urgency theatre — measured null effect.
